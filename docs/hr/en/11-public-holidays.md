# Public holidays

This page is about the list of days on which nobody in the company is expected to work. It is for whoever keeps that list — usually the office administrator — and for anybody who has looked at their own month and wondered why a public holiday did, or did not, change their figures. It answers what is already on the calendar, how to add a day the company grants, how to make a day count as half, how to take a day off the list, and exactly what any of that does to the hours somebody owes.

## Where the screen is

There is no link to it in the left-hand sidebar. You get there through the team's month.

1. In the left-hand sidebar, open **My time**. Your own month appears.
2. At the top right of that screen, open the **⋯** menu and choose **Everyone's hours**. This entry only appears if you look after the team. You land on **Team time**.
3. In the row of links along the top of **Team time** — **Hours in detail**, **People**, **Absences**, **Public holidays**, **Bring earlier records in** — click **Public holidays**.

The line at the top of the screen you land on reads:

> **The days nobody works. A holiday counts as hours worked; it does not lower what somebody has to work. A change here only reaches months that are still open.**

That one sentence is the whole rule. The rest of this page is what it means in practice.

## Who may change it

Anybody who is set up in the time records can open this screen and read the list. That is deliberate: everybody needs to know which days are holidays.

Changing the list is another matter. Only somebody who looks after the team can add a day, change a day's length or remove one.

The screen does not currently hide the buttons from anyone. If you are not a manager, you will still see the **Add** form and the icons at the end of each row, and they will still look as though they work. When you press one, nothing is saved and a message appears reading **That could not be saved**, with the words _You don't have the required permissions._ underneath. Nothing was written. Ask whoever looks after the team to make the change instead of pressing it again.

If you are not set up in the time records at all, the screen does not load. You see **This is not yours to see** and, under it, **You are not set up in the time records yet, so this list is not shown to you. Ask whoever looks after the team.**

## What is already on the calendar

Austria's thirteen public holidays are already there for each year the calendar covers, so in an ordinary year the list holds fifteen rows:

| Day                   | Date              | Kind    | Length    |
| --------------------- | ----------------- | ------- | --------- |
| New Year's Day        | 1 January         | By law  | Whole day |
| Epiphany              | 6 January         | By law  | Whole day |
| Easter Monday         | moves with Easter | By law  | Whole day |
| State Holiday         | 1 May             | By law  | Whole day |
| Ascension Day         | moves with Easter | By law  | Whole day |
| Whit Monday           | moves with Easter | By law  | Whole day |
| Corpus Christi        | moves with Easter | By law  | Whole day |
| Assumption of Mary    | 15 August         | By law  | Whole day |
| National Day          | 26 October        | By law  | Whole day |
| All Saints' Day       | 1 November        | By law  | Whole day |
| Immaculate Conception | 8 December        | By law  | Whole day |
| Christmas Day         | 25 December       | By law  | Whole day |
| St Stephen's Day      | 26 December       | By law  | Whole day |
| Christmas Eve         | 24 December       | Granted | Half day  |
| New Year's Eve        | 31 December       | Granted | Half day  |

Good Friday is not on the list. It is not a general public holiday in Austria, and it is left off on purpose.

Christmas Eve and New Year's Eve are on the list as half days because that is what most companies agree, not because any law says so. They are marked **Granted**, and either of them can be made a whole day or removed like any other granted day.

Anything else the company decides to close for — a bridging day, an office closure — is not there until somebody types it in.

## The year on screen

The list shows one year at a time. The year is in the middle of the row above the **Add** panel, with an arrow on each side: **Previous year** on the left, **Next year** on the right. Click an arrow and the list below reloads for that year.

The year you arrive on is the current one, taken from the computer you are sitting at.

Neither arrow ever stops. You can step forward or back as far as you like, and years the calendar does not cover show as empty.

Most of the time there is nothing to choose above the year. A **Calendar** picker appears there only if the company has more than one holiday calendar set up, which it normally does not. With one calendar, everybody is on it.

## Reading the list

The days are shown oldest first, one row each.

| Column     | What it shows                                                                                                                                                              |
| ---------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Date**   | The day itself, with its year, for example `26 Oct 2027`.                                                                                                                  |
| **Name**   | What the day is called.                                                                                                                                                    |
| **Kind**   | **By law, cannot be changed** for one of Austria's thirteen, or **Granted** for a day the company decided on.                                                              |
| **Length** | **Whole day** or **Half day**.                                                                                                                                             |
| **Action** | The buttons for changing the row. A row marked **By law, cannot be changed** has none: those days are not the company's to shorten or to take away, and the cell is empty. |

There is no search, no sorting and no count. Fifteen rows a year is short enough to read.

## What a holiday does to the hours somebody owes

This is the part that surprises people, so it is worth reading even if nothing looks wrong.

A holiday does **not** reduce **To work**. It adds to **Worked**.

On **My time** and on **Team time** there are two figures side by side: **To work**, which is what somebody's contract says they owe for the month, and **Worked**. A public holiday leaves **To work** exactly as it was and puts the day's hours into **Worked** instead, as though the person had worked them. The **Balance** — **Worked** minus **To work** — comes out the same as it would have done either way, which is why nobody is better or worse off for it.

It is done this way so that the hours figure sent to the payroll accountant still contains the hours the person is actually paid for. If holidays were quietly taken off **To work**, the hours column would be short by every holiday in the month.

Where it applies, **My time** says so in small text under the figures: **includes {duration} away or on holiday**. That figure is time off and public holidays added together.

### Where the number comes from

A **Whole day** is worth that person's own scheduled hours for that particular weekday. It is not a fixed number, and the screen cannot show one, because it is different for everybody:

- Somebody whose Tuesdays are 7:42 long is credited 7:42 for a Tuesday holiday.
- Somebody whose Tuesdays are 4:00 long is credited 4:00.
- Somebody who does not work Tuesdays is credited nothing.

A **Half day** is worth half of that, rounded to the nearest whole minute, with a half-minute rounded up. Half of a 7:42 day is 3:51. Half of a 7:43 day is 3:52, not 3:51.

So a single row on this screen is a different amount of time for each of ten people, and for some of them it is none at all. That is normal.

## A holiday on a day somebody never works

A public holiday is only worth what the person would have worked that day. On a day they never work it is worth nothing, and nothing has been taken from them — there were no hours there to take.

Two things follow, and both look wrong at first glance.

**When a holiday falls at the weekend, nobody on a Monday-to-Friday week gets anything for it.** All Saints' Day, 1 November 2026, is a Sunday. It sits on the calendar as **By law, cannot be changed / Whole day**, and it is worth 0:00 to everybody who works Monday to Friday. Nothing is carried to another day and nothing is owed back. In Austria a public holiday is a day you are not required to work, not a day off you are given; if it lands on a day you were not required to work anyway, there is nothing to release.

**The same applies to somebody's own days off.** Sofia works Tuesday to Friday and never Mondays. National Day, 26 October 2026, is a Monday. Anna, who works Monday to Friday, is credited 7:42 for it. Sofia is credited 0:00 for the same day.

On the day-by-day list on **My time**, the column **What it was** shows this as **Not a working day**, not as **Public holiday**. That wording wins over everything else, because on a day nobody was expected to work, calling it a public holiday would invite the reader to look for hours that were never owed.

## People who invoice their own hours

Two of the four employment arrangements here are self-invoicing. For those people, no hours are expected of them each day, no running balance is kept, and a public holiday is worth nothing at all. It is not an oversight: holiday pay goes with an obligation to work set hours, and somebody invoicing us for their own hours does not have one.

On **People**, the setting that decides this is **Expect set hours each day, keep a running balance, and count the leave they take**. When it is off, holidays do nothing for that person.

This matters after you grant a company day. You add the day, you look at **Team time** to check it landed, and two people's figures have not moved at all. That is correct. The day did save. Do not book the hours in by hand to make the columns agree — that would pay somebody for a holiday they are not owed, and it would be very hard to spot later.

## Half days

A **Half day** credits half the person's scheduled hours for that weekday and leaves the other half still to work. It does not shorten the day's **To work**.

For somebody on 7:42 days, Christmas Eve as a **Half day** credits 3:51. If they do no other work that day, that day ends 3:51 short, and the month's **Balance** is 3:51 lower than it would otherwise have been. That is the intended result: half the day was a holiday and the other half was a working half-day.

If the company decides that this year 24 December is closed entirely, make the row a **Whole day** — see below. Do not delete it.

On the day-by-day list, a half holiday with nothing else on it shows as **Half holiday**. If time off covers the other half of the day, it shows as **Partly away**.

## A holiday and time off on the same day

The holiday takes its share of the day first. Time off can only draw on what is left.

- **A half holiday and a half day of leave on the same day.** For somebody on 7:42 days, 24 December credits 3:51 as a holiday. A half day of leave that afternoon costs 3:51 of their leave, not a whole day, and the day comes out level. Booking leave on 24 December costs half a day of entitlement, not one.
- **A week of leave with a public holiday in it.** The holiday pays for its own day, and only the remaining four days come off the person's leave. Their leave is charged four days, not five.

There is a trap in that second case. On the **Absences** screen the column **Hours away** shows how long the absence runs against the person's working week, and it does not know about public holidays — a week of leave containing one still reads as a whole week there. Both figures are right for what they measure. What the leave actually cost is the leave figure on **My time**, not the **Hours away** column. "Time away from work" and "Leave" go into this properly.

## A worked example: Anna's October

Anna works Monday to Friday and owes **7:42** each of those days. October 2027 has 21 days she is scheduled to work. National Day, Tuesday 26 October, is on the calendar as **By law, cannot be changed / Whole day**. She takes two days of leave in the same month.

**What she owes for the month**

```
21 scheduled days × 7:42  =  161:42     ← To work
```

The holiday does not change this figure. Neither do the two days of leave. **To work** is 161:42 whatever else happens in the month.

**What is credited to her without her being at a desk**

```
public holiday   1 day × 7:42  =    7:42
leave            2 days × 7:42 =   15:24
                                  -------
                                    23:06
```

**What she has to record herself**

```
161:42  −  23:06  =  138:36
```

**What the month shows when she has done exactly that**

```
To work   161:42
Worked    161:42     (138:36 recorded + 7:42 holiday + 15:24 leave)
          includes 23:06 away or on holiday
Balance     0:00
```

The 23:06 she never sat at a desk for is inside **Worked**, not taken off **To work**. That is the rule at the top of this page, in figures.

**The same month for Sofia**, who works Tuesday to Friday on 6:00 days: her **To work** for October 2027 is 17 days × 6:00 = 102:00, and Tuesday 26 October credits her 6:00. Same row on the calendar, different amount.

## Adding a day the company grants

Use this for a day the company itself decides to close: a bridging day, an office closure between Christmas and New Year, an anniversary. Anything you add here is recorded as **Granted**, never as **By law, cannot be changed** — a day the company grants is not a statutory holiday and must not claim to be one.

1. Check the year shown above the **Add a day off for everyone** panel. If the day is in a different year, you can still type it; the screen moves to the right year by itself after saving.
2. Click into **Date** and pick the day.
3. Type what it is called into **Name**. The box shows **What the day is called** until you do. Give it a name a colleague would recognise on a list a year later — "Office closed" rather than "closure".
4. If the office is closed for only half of that day, tick **Only half a day**. Leave it unticked for a whole day off.
5. Press **Add**. Pressing Enter in either box does the same thing.

You will see a green message reading **Day added**, the boxes empty themselves, and the new row appears in the list in date order, marked **Granted**. If the day you typed was in another year, the year above the list changes to that year so you can see it.

Whatever you type as the name is used for both the English and the German screens, so a German-speaking colleague sees exactly the words you typed. There is no way to translate it afterwards.

## Making a day half, or a whole day again

This is the only part of a day that can be changed in place, and only on rows marked **Granted**.

There is no confirmation step. One click changes the day for everybody in every month that is still open — for ten people on 7:42 days, that is close to four hours each, nearly forty hours across the company. Be sure of the year on screen before you click.

1. Find the row. Check the **Date** column, including its year.
2. At the end of the row, click the first icon. Its label says what it will do: **Make it half a day** on a row that is currently a whole day, **Make it a whole day** on a row that is currently half. Hovering shows the label with the day's name after it, for example _Make it a whole day — Christmas Eve_.
3. The **Length** cell changes, the button's label flips to the opposite, and a short green message appears with the day's name and its new length — **Whole day** or **Half day**.

If nothing appears and you get **That could not be saved** instead, nothing was changed. See [Messages you may see](#messages-you-may-see).

Days marked **By law, cannot be changed** have no such button. Halving one would quietly raise everybody's hours for a day the law says they need not work.

## Removing a day

**A removed day cannot be brought back.** There is no undo. You can type the day in again, but it returns as **Granted**, and nothing anywhere records that it was ever removed or by whom.

Only days marked **Granted** can be removed. Days marked **By law, cannot be changed** have no remove button at all.

1. Find the row and check its **Date**, including the year.
2. Click the bin icon at the end of the row. Hovering shows _Remove this day —_ and the day's name.
3. A box opens headed **Remove this day?**, saying **{day} stops being a holiday. Everybody who works that day is expected to work it again.**
4. To go ahead, press the red **Remove this day**. It reads **Removing…** while it works. To change your mind, press **Cancel**, press Escape, or click outside the box; nothing is changed.

A green message reads **Day removed** and the row disappears. Everybody who works that weekday loses that day's credit at the next recalculation of their month, so their **Balance** falls by a day's hours.

## Correcting a day that was typed wrongly

There is no way to edit a day. The length is the only part that can be changed in place.

If you typed the wrong date or the wrong name, the only route is to remove the day and add it again. Do both in the same visit, so the calendar is never left wrong overnight. The dialog's warning about everybody having to work that day is written for somebody genuinely cancelling a day off; when you are putting the same day straight back with a corrected spelling, it does not apply.

A day set **By law, cannot be changed** cannot be corrected or removed here at all. If one of the thirteen is wrong, that is a question for whoever installed the system.

## When the change reaches the figures

Nothing recalculates the moment you press a button here. The calendar row changes at once; people's monthly figures change the next time their month is worked out.

A person's month is worked out again when:

- they open **My time** for that month;
- somebody opens **Team time** for that month;
- they press **Bring up to date** on **My time**;
- the month is handed in, agreed or closed;
- or the scheduled hourly run happens, twenty minutes past every hour, which covers the current month and the one before it.

So if you grant a day in a month several months back, nothing you can see moves until somebody navigates to that month. That is not a failure to save. "The month, from open to closed" explains this in full.

## Months that have already been handed in or closed

A change made here **only reaches months that are still open**. That is the third sentence of the line at the top of the screen, and it is the thing most worth remembering.

A month that has been handed in, agreed or closed is never worked out again. It keeps the figures it had, whatever the calendar says afterwards. Hours, absences and attendance dated into such a month are refused outright with a message saying so. Holidays are not: you can add, halve or remove a day sitting inside a closed month, you will get the green **Day added** or **Day removed**, and nobody's closed month will move a minute. The calendar and the figures the accountant already has then disagree, quietly.

If a correction genuinely has to reach a settled month, the order is:

1. Ask whoever looks after the team to reopen that person's month on **Team time**.
2. Make the change here.
3. Open that month again so it is worked out afresh, and check the figures.
4. Have the month handed in and closed again.

Also worth knowing: no change made on this screen is recorded anywhere. Employment records keep a trail of who changed what; holidays do not. There is no answer to "who took that day off the calendar, and when". If you make a change that matters, say so to whoever looks after the team in writing.

## Keeping the year ahead stocked

The statutory days were filled in for a stretch of years when the system was installed — the year of installation, five years back and five years forward. Nothing extends that range by itself. There is no reminder, no warning, and no button.

When the range runs out, the screen does not break. It simply shows, for that year:

> **No days for this year yet. Until they are added, every public holiday in it counts as an ordinary working day.**

That is exactly what happens. Thirteen days a year become ordinary working days for everybody, and each one shows as a full day's shortfall against people who were legally off.

**Make this a job every autumn.** Open **Public holidays**, press **Next year** until you reach the year after the one you are in, and count the rows. Fifteen in an ordinary year — thirteen **By law, cannot be changed** plus Christmas Eve and New Year's Eve as **Granted / Half day**. If the year is empty or short, either type the missing days in here by hand, or ask whoever installed the system to run the one-off setup step again. Running it again is safe: it fills in five years either side and leaves alone any day that is already there.

One caution if the setup step is re-run: a statutory day that somebody removed by hand will come back, because the step puts back any date it does not find. A day whose length or name was edited is left as it is.
